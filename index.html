I am a molecular biologist. My work sits at the junction of molecular evolution, comparative genomics and epigenetics, in the field of gene silencing and activation in metazoans.

The question I keep returning to is how eukaryotic cells recognize and repress foreign genetic material: transposable elements, and genes picked up from bacteria by horizontal gene transfer. I am equally interested in what happens when a piece of that foreign machinery is turned into a tool of the host's own genome defense.

Much of that work has been done in bdelloid rotifers, microscopic freshwater invertebrates with transposon-poor genomes unusually rich in genes of bacterial origin, alongside comparative work in other invertebrates and in the chamois genus Rupicapra.

Current lines of work

01

Horizontal gene transfer as evolutionary innovation

What foreign genes do once they arrive in an animal genome, including a bacterial methyltransferase that became part of the host's silencing machinery.

02

Reverse-transcriptase-related genes

Penelope-like retroelements, permuted domain architectures, and giant retroelements that sit at chromosome ends.

03

Mobile elements in sexual and asexual taxa

How transposon landscapes and the silencing pathways that contain them differ in lineages that have gone without sex for a very long time.
